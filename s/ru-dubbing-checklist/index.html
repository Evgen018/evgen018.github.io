Where Russian AI dubbing breaks in outdoor video

A checklist from a native speaker, written for hunting, fishing and nature narration. Every item below survives a spell check and a grammar check, and every one of them is audible to a Russian ear in the first minute. Use it freely with whoever reviews your episodes.

1. Stress that changes the word

Russian writes these pairs identically and tells them apart only by stress. A synthesiser has to guess, and it guesses from statistics rather than from the sentence.

2. Ё read as Е

Russian print usually drops the two dots over ё, so the text gives the voice no hint. This is the error viewers feel without being able to name it, and a non-native reviewer does not hear it at all. Outdoor vocabulary is full of it.

One counter-example worth knowing, because it runs the other way: when a pointing dog freezes on a bird, the Russian term really is «сто́йка». A reviewer who has learned to delete «стойка» everywhere will break this line instead of fixing it.

5. Numbers and units

A judgement call rather than an error, and one the client should make once for the whole catalogue rather than episode by episode.

Russian outdoor channels are metric. «Триста ярдов» is a correct translation of 300 yards and still sounds foreign to the audience, because nobody in the field speaks that way. The same goes for pounds and inches. Converting is not always right either: in a story about an American hunt the yards can be part of the setting. What matters is that one rule is chosen and then held across every episode.

6. Why a grammar check will not find any of this

Every line above is spelled correctly, is grammatically valid and would pass any automatic proofreader. Stress is not written down in Russian at all; ё is optional in print; and a literal term translation is a perfectly normal word in the wrong place. These errors exist only in the sound and only for someone who grew up with it.
